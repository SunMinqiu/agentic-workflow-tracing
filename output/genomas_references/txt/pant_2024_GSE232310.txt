IMMUNOLOGY
Monocytes in type 1 diabetes families exhibit high
cytolytic activity and subset abundances that correlate
with clinical progression
Tarun Pant1,2, Chien- Wei Lin3, Amina Bedrat1,2, Shuang Jia1,2, Mark F. Roethle1,2,
Nathan A. Truchan1,2, Ashley E. Ciecko1,2, Yi- Guang Chen1,2, Martin J. Hessner1,2*
Monocytes are immune regulators implicated in the pathogenesis of type 1 diabetes (T1D), an autoimmune disease
that targets insulin- producing pancreatic β cells. We determined that monocytes of recent onset (RO) T1D patients and
their healthy siblings express proinflammatory/cytolytic transcriptomes and hypersecrete cytokines in response
to lipopolysaccharide exposure compared to unrelated healthy controls (uHCs). Flow cytometry measured elevated
circulating abundances of intermediate monocytes and >2- fold more CD14+CD16+HLADR+KLRD1+PRF1+ NK- like
monocytes among patients with ROT1D compared to uHC. The intermediate to nonclassical monocyte ratio among
ROT1D patients correlated with the decline in functional β cell mass during the first 24 months after onset. Among
sibling nonprogressors, temporal decreases were measured in the intermediate to nonclassical monocyte ratio
and NK- like monocyte abundances; these changes coincided with increases in activated regulatory T cells. In contrast, these monocyte populations exhibited stability among T1D progressors. This study associates heightened
monocyte proinflammatory/cytolytic activity with T1D susceptibility and progression and offers insight to the
age- dependent decline in T1D susceptibility.
INTRODUCTION
Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a heterogeneous autoimmune disease that
results in the loss of pancreatic β cell functional mass and lifelong
dependence on exogenous insulin replacement therapy. As evidenced by the partial successes of immunotherapies targeting T cells
(1, 2), T1D pathogenesis involves adaptive immune responses. However, a growing body of evidence supports a role for altered innate
immune responses and inflammation in T1D pathogenesis (3-6).
Monocytes are innate immune cells that comprise ~ 10% of the
nucleated blood cell count. They are often classified into three phenotypically and functionally distinct subgroups based on the relative expression of CD14 [lipopolysaccharide (LPS) receptor] and
CD16 [Fc gamma receptor III (Fcγ RIII)] (7). Classical monocytes
(CD14+CD16−), which comprise 80 to 85% of the peripheral pool
in heathy individuals, are efficient phagocytes and can secrete
inflammation- stimulating factors (8). A portion of classical monocytes differentiate into the intermediate subtype (CD14 +CD16+),
which comprise 2 to 8% of the circulating pool. They are characterized by higher major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II
expression and function in antigen presentation, T cell stimulation,
and the production of reactive oxygen species. Intermediate monocytes
can further differentiate into the nonclassical subset (CD14dimCD16+),
which comprise 2 to 11% of circulating monocytes. While nonclassical monocytes can function in a proinflammatory capacity and
liberate interleukin- 1 (IL- 1), tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and
IL- 12 in response to infection, they also function in the resolution
of inflammation (9 , 10), maintenance of vascular homeostasis,
and the clearance of dying cells, viruses, and tumor cells from the
circulation (11).
Monocytes have been implicated in the pathology of numerous
inflammatory and autoimmune diseases (12). Expansions of inter mediate monocytes have been reported in Graves’ disease (13),
rheumatoid arthritis (14), and anti- neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated vasculitis (15), while nonclassical monocytes have
been implicated in relapsing- remitting multiple sclerosis, coronary
artery disease, and systemic lupus erythematosus (11). Compared to
unrelated healthy controls (uHCs), Ren et al. (16) reported that patients with recent onset T1D (ROT1D) had a greater abundance of
circulating intermediate monocytes that was positively associated
with hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and negatively associated with serum insulin and C- peptide levels. Further, Bradshaw et al. (17) re ported that monocytes of patients with T1D spontaneously secrete
IL- 1β and IL- 6, inflammatory cytokines known to induce and expand T helper 17 (TH17) cells, suggesting that abnormal innate immune activity may drive adaptive responses in T1D by expanding
the TH17 population of effector T cells.
The advancement of single- cell RNA sequencing (scRNA- seq)
has provided insight into the cellular heterogeneity of the immune
system. Using this approach, Villani et al. (18) described four monocyte subsets in healthy human participants, where the intermediate
monocyte subset was separated into two groups, designated mono3
and mono4 ( 18). Notably, the mono4 subset expressed a cytolytic
transcriptome, which included high expression of perforin, granzymes, and granulysin. Using scRNA- seq, a similar monocyte subset
has also been identified by Hu et al. (19).
On the basis of the work summarized above, as well as our own
studies (3, 20, 21), we hypothesized that altered monocyte abundances
and activity may be associated with T1D progression. We therefore
examined monocytes isolated from patients with ROT1D, healthy
autoantibody- negative siblings of probands, and uHC. Monocytes
of T1D family members were found to hypersecrete cytokines upon

Monocytes of T1D family members hypersecrete cytokines
in response to TLR4 ligation
Hussain et al. (22) reported that peripheral blood mononuclear cell
(PBMC) isolated from patients with T1D and their healthy firstdegree relatives hypersecrete IL- 1α and TNF- α after mitogen stimulation. To further characterize innate immune function in T1D
families, we isolated monocytes from cryopreserved PBMCs drawn
from ROT1D, autoantibody- negative siblings of probands with highrisk (DR3 and/or DR4) human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genotypes
(HRS) or with lower risk (non- DR3/DR4) HLA genotypes (LRS), and
uHC. The characteristics of the study participants are provided in
table S1. Monocytes were cultured in the presence of LPS, and the
secretion of IL- 1β, IL- 6, and TNF- α was measured in the conditioned
medium after 24 hours. Levels of cytokine secretion were not different among the patients with ROT1D and the autoantibody- negative
healthy sibling cohorts. By contrast, LPS- stimulated IL- 1β, IL- 6, and
TNF- α secretion was elevated in ROT1D monocytes, as well as
the normoglycemic HRS and LRS cohorts when compared to uHC
(P < 0.05; Fig. 1, A to C).

To further characterize the 826 probe sets differently regulated
among ROT1D, HRS, LRS, and uHC monocytes, Gene Ontology
(GO) analyses were conducted (Fig. 2D). Consistent with prior reports that have associated increased abundances of intermediate
monocytes with T1D (16), the ROT1D monocytes expressed a transcriptional signature consistent with antigen presentation/T cell costimulation (CD160, GZMA, ITK, LCK, NFATC2, NLRC3, RORA,
SLAMF6, SLAMF7, and ZAP70). The ROT1D monocyte signature
was also enriched in transcripts related to natural killer (NK) cell
activation (GATA3, IL2RB, IL18R1, IL21R, KIR2DS2, KIR3DS1,
NCR1, NCR3, and SH2D1A) and NK cytotoxicity (CD7, CD247,
CTSW, FASLG, GNLY, GZMB, IL18RAP, KIR3DL2, KLRB1, KLRD1,
NKG7, PRF1, and SH2D1B). A similar, albeit less robust signature
was observed in the healthy sibling cohorts. The cytolytic ROT1D
signature overlapped with that described for the mono4 intermediate subset defined by Villani et al. (18), which also exhibited high
expression of PRF1, GZMB, GNLY, CTSW, and KLRD1. Specifically,
Villani et al. (18) identified 149 transcripts associated with mono4;
132 of these were represented on the Affymetrix U133 plus 2 array,
and 69 of 132 (52.3%; χ2, P < 1.0 ×10−5) were among the 826 transcripts differentially expressed among monocytes of the uHC, LRS,
HRS, and ROT1D cohorts.
scRNA- seq analysis of ROT1D, HRS, LRS, and uHC monocytes
Because scRNA- seq has fostered the identification of monocyte
populations extending beyond the traditional tripartite classification
scheme (18, 19), we applied it to monocyte- enriched PBMC populations of uHC, LRS, HRS, and ROT1D study participants. For these
eight participants (two per cohort), 40,000 cells were sequenced at a
depth of 500 to 5000 transcripts per cell. The 2000 genes exhibiting
the highest expression variability among these cells were subjected to
principal components analysis (PCA). For visualization, the top 50
significant PCs were reduced to two dimensions using t- distributed
stochastic neighbor embedding (t- SNE); this unsupervised analysis
identified 13 clusters (Fig. 3A). Differential expression analysis was
performed for each cluster (table S3), and the identity of each cluster
was defined by comparing significantly associated marker gene
expression (adjusted P < 0.001) to that of established reference clusters in blood samples. The following putative populations were
identified: CD14 + monocytes, NK cells, naïve CD4 + T cells,
memory CD4 + T cells, naïve CD8+ T cells, NKT cells, B cells, CD16+
monocytes, memory CD8 + T cells, γδ T cells, CD4 + regulatory
T (Treg) cells, CD1c dendritic cells, and plasmacytoid dendritic cells
(Fig. 3, A to C).
The CD14high monocyte cluster exhibited high expression of numerous transcripts previously associated with the classical and intermediate subsets (CD14, CSTA, CTSS, CXCL8, MNDA, MS4A6A,
S100A8, S100A9, S100A12, VCAN, LGALS1, LGALS2, and LYZ)
(19). The CD16 high monocyte cluster exhibited high expression of
transcripts previously associated with the nonclassical subset
(FCGR3A, FCGR3B, TCF7L2, MS4A7, RHOC, IFITM3, CD79B,
CSF1R, CDKN1C, HES4, SMIM25, MTSS1, BCL2A1, and RRAS )
(19). We examined the transcriptional profiles across the clusters
and detected gene expression consistent with antigen presentation
(HLA- DPA1, HLA- DPB1, HLA- DQB1, HLA- DRB1, and HLA- DMA)
by monocytes as well as B cells and dendritic cells. Expected gene
expression patterns were also observed for NK cells [KLRD1,
KLRF1, NKG7, and NCAM1(CD56)], naïve CD4 + T cells (CCR7,
FHIT, and CD4), memory CD4 + T cells (TNFRSF4, IL7R, and
CD40LG), naïve CD8+ T cells (CD8b and LEF1), NKT cells (NCR3
and KLRB1/CD161), B cells (CD79A and CD79B), memory CD8 +
T cells (CD8A, CCL5, and IL32), γδ T cells (TRGC2 and NUCB2),
CD4+ Treg cells (FOXP3, CTLA4, and IL2RA), CD1c dendritic cells
(CD1C, CLEC10A, and PDL4 ), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells
(CLSPN, RAD51AP1, and STMN1) (18, 19, 24).
The CD14high and CD16 high monocyte clusters illustrated in
Fig. 3 totaled 13,915 cells; these were subjected to further analysis.
The top 10 significant PCs were used to reduce the data to two dimensions and visualized by Uniform Manifold Approximation and
Projection (UMAP). High heterogeneity was observed as evidenced
by the identification of 10 clusters (Fig. 4A), all of which expressed
CD14 and/or CD16 as well as HLA- DRB1 (Fig. 4, A to C). Azimuth
analysis predicted that 99.11% (13,791 of 13,915) of these cells were
monocytes (25). The clusters were characterized by their top marker
genes (table S4) and through ontological analysis to identify their
associated GO biological processes and molecular functions
(table S5). Cluster 3 exhibited the highest CD14 expression, low
CD16 expression, and consistent with studies of sorted classical
monocytes (23) and expressed the highest levels of EIF2AK2, IFI44,
IFI44L, MX1, and other transcripts related to antiviral and innate
immune responses. Cluster 5 exhibited the highest CD16 and
lowest CD14 abundance transcript levels, as well as the highest levels
of previously reported marker genes for nonclassical monocytes
(FCGR3A, CDKN1C, HES4, TCF7L2, RHOC, and MTSS1) (23, 26).
In line with this observation, in silico pseudotime trajectory analysis of the 10 clusters (fig. S2) found cluster 5 the most differentiated.
The remaining clusters expressed varying levels of CD14 and CD16,
suggesting differing transition states from classical to nonclassical
monocytes. Paralleling the S100A12 subset described by Hu et al.
(19), cluster 0 expressed high levels of the proinflammatory calgranulin genes S100A8, S100A9, and S100A12. In line with the high

most distinguishing feature of cluster 1 and was consistent with the

GO Term. The top marker genes for cluster 2 included CLEC4E,

Flow cytometric analysis of monocyte populations
To confirm and extend the transcriptomic data, we began by examining the abundances of classical, intermediate, and nonclassical
monocytes in the cross- sectional cohorts by flow cytometry after
surface staining for CD14 and CD16 (Fig. 6A). Because hyperglycemia is known to affect many immune and inflammatory markers,
these analyses included long- standing (LS) T1D patient controls
(age, 12.7 ± 5.4 years after onset) that exhibited glycemic control
similar to the ROT1D cohort (HbA1c, 8.5 ± 1.0% versus 7.8 ± 1.3%,
respectively; P > 0.05, two- tailed t test). We measured a higher
abundance of classical CD14+CD16− monocytes among total monocytes when comparing patients with ROT1D to uHC (85.3% versus
76.4%, P = 0.01) and patients with ROT1D to LST1D patients
(85.3% versus 74.1%, P = 0.0001); classical CD14+CD16− monocytes
were also more abundant when comparing HRS to uHC (84.7%
versus 76.4%, P = 0.04; Fig. 6B). Consistent with Ren et al. (16), we
measured a higher abundance of intermediate CD14+CD16+ monocytes among total monocytes when comparing patients with ROT1D
to uHC (10.5% versus 6.4%, P = 0.01). The abundance of intermediate CD14+CD16+ monocytes among total monocytes in the LST1D
cohort was similar to the ROT1D group (12.4% versus 10.5%,
P > 0.05); however, this population was more abundant among
patients with LST1D compared to HRS, LRS, and uHC (Fig. 6B).
The abundances of classical and intermediate monocytes in the LRS
and HRS groups were between the levels measured for the ROT1D
and uHC groups. The percentages of nonclassical CD14 dimCD16+
monocytes among total monocytes were lower among patients with
ROT1D compared to uHC (4.1% versus 17.2%, P = 0.0001); they
were also lower when comparing the healthy siblings to uHC (LRS
versus uHC: 9.1% versus 17.2%, P = 0.025; HRS versus uHC: 6.8%
versus 17.2%, P = 0.001) (Fig. 6B). The patients with LST1D exhibited percentages of nonclassical CD14dimCD16+ monocytes among
total monocytes that were similar to the uHC patients and notably
higher than HRS and patients with ROT1D (LST1D versus HRS: 13.3%
versus 6.8%, P = 0.043; LST1D versus ROT1D: 13.3% versus 4.1%,
P = 0.0003). NK cells were defined as being CD3−CD19−CD56+CD14−,
and their abundances among total lymphocytes across the five cohorts did not significantly differ (Fig. 6C).
To identify and enumerate mono4 monocytes, we developed an
antibody panel to target surface and intracellular proteins that were
among the top cluster 9 marker genes (Fig. 4C), as well as among the
highly expressed transcripts of (i) the negatively selected ROT1D
monocytes (Fig. 2E), (ii) the mono4 population described by Villani
et al. (18), and (iii) the NK- like monocyte population described by
Hu et al. (19). We selected as cell- surface targets the lectin- like receptors KLRD1 (CD94) and KLRB1 (CD161). KLRD1 expression by
NK cells is associated with effector function and cell survival and is
linked to innate and adaptive immune responses to pathogens (30).
Notably, KLRB1 expression identifies a proinflammatory NK cell population that exhibits high cytokine responsiveness and may contribute
to inflammatory disease pathogenesis (31). We selected the cytolytic
protein perforin (PRF1) as an intracellular target. Monocytes of
uHC, LRS, HRS, and ROT1D study participants were analyzed by
flow cytometry using an antibody panel that targeted CD14, CD16,
CD3, CD19, CD56, HLA- DR, KLRB1, KLRD1, and PRF1 (Fig. 7A).

We found that the transcriptomes of uHC monocytes positively
correlated with the gene expression profiles of sorted classical
monocytes, while transcriptomes of ROT1D monocytes positively
correlated with the gene expression profiles of sorted nonclassical
monocytes (Fig. 2C). Consistent with this observation, the lowest
percentage of KLRD1, KLRB1, and PRF1 expressing classical and
intermediate monocytes was observed in the uHC cohort. Overall,
these data were consistent with transcriptomic analyses in that they
showed significantly higher mono4 abundances in patients with
ROT1D and detected the mono4/NK- like population distributed
across the classical, intermediate, and nonclassical subsets.
Relationships between monocyte populations and β cell
function among patients with ROT1D
Mixed meal tolerance tests (MMTTs) permit calculation of stimulated C- peptide AUC (area under the curve), the most widely
accepted measure of endogenous insulin production (32). Relationships between abundances of classical and mono4 monocytes
and stimulated C- peptide AUC were not detected among patients
with ROT1D. However, we did identify a negative relationship between the baseline percentage of intermediate monocytes and the
rate (slope) of C- peptide decline over the 24- month study period
[(r (correlation coefficient) = −0.45, P = 0.03] (Fig. 8A). We also
identified a positive relationship between the baseline percentage
of nonclassical monocytes and the rate (slope) of C- peptide decline
over the 24- month study period (r = 0.46, P = 0.03) (Fig. 8B). Over
the study period, robust relationships between HbA1c or insulin
dose and the abundances of classical, intermediate, nonclassical, or
mono4 monocytes were not detected. However, positive relationships were measured between (i) the baseline HbA1c and baseline KLRD1 high/PRF1high mono4 monocyte abundances as well as
(ii) the 6- month insulin dose and baseline KLRD1 high/KLRB1high
mono4 monocyte abundances. A negative relationship was observed
between the 24- month insulin dose and baseline nonclassical
monocytes abundances (table S6).
Next, we examined the ratio between the percentage of intermediate monocytes versus the percentage of nonclassical monocytes.
There was a large ratio range for the ROT1D group (Fig. 8C) that
was, on average, higher than uHC (P = 0.02) and LST1D (P = 0.02).
Notably among the ROT1D group, an inverse relationship was observed between the intermediate- to- nonclassical monocyte ratio at
baseline and the percent change from baseline C- peptide AUC at 6,
12, and 24 months (P < 0.02; Fig. 8D). Similarly, the intermediateto- nonclassical monocyte ratio at baseline was inversely related to
the rate (slope) of C- peptide decline over the 24- month study period (r = −0.62, P < 0.002) (Fig. 8E). Together, these data support
the hypothesis that (i) individuals with a higher intermediate- tononclassical monocyte ratio at onset will experience accelerated
decline in β cell function; (ii) that intermediate monocytes promote
more rapid post- onset progression; and (iii) nonclassical monocytes may be functioning in an immunoregulatory capacity to slow
post- onset progression.
Longitudinal analysis of monocyte and Treg populations in
nonprogressors and T1D progressors
We previously reported that the circulating percentage of activated
CD4+CD45RA −FoxP3high T reg increased in an age- dependent
manner among HRS nonprogressors, suggesting an immunoregulatory mechanism underlying the decline in T1D susceptibility
with age (29). However, among T1D progressors, the abundance of
this highly suppressive subset remained stable. Using flow cytometry, we longitudinally examined PBMCs of nonprogressors and
progressors for changes in circulating activated T reg and monocyte
abundances to better understand the temporal dynamics of these
two populations.
Twenty- two HRS nonprogressors (mean age of 8.2 ± 3.5 years
at the first sampling) were examined at two time points (mean
duration of 5.4 ± 1.3 years between samplings). These stage 0
participants were either antibody negative at both time points
(n = 19) or positive for a single antibody at one time point
(n = 3). Between time points 1 and 2, there were changes in the
abundances of classical (65.8 to 77.2%; P = 0.001), intermediate
(20.9 to 13.8%; P = 0.015), and nonclassical monocytes (12.9
to 9.2%; P = 0.061) as well as changes in the intermediate: nonclassical ratio (3.5 to 2.0; P = 0.045) (Fig. 9A). Mono4 monocytes
were defined as CD14 + and/or CD16 +, HLA- DR+, KLRD1 high ,
KLRB1high, and PRF1high. For these longitudinal analyses, the antibody panel was expanded to include intracellular staining for the
cytolytic protein granzyme B (GZMB). Among classical, intermediate, and nonclassical monocytes of nonprogressors, significant temporal decreases in the percentage of KLRD1highKLRB1high (−41.5%,
P = 0.006; −38.5%, P = 0.009; and −17.3%, P = 0.031, respectively;
Fig. 9B), KLRD1 high PRF1 high ( −15.8%, P = 0.014; −59.2%,
P = 0.049; and −0.42%, P = 0.019, respectively; Fig. 9C), and
KLRD1 high GZMB high ( −29.1%, P = 0.045; −32.8%, P = 0.004;
and −13.5%, P = 0.031, respectively; Fig. 9D) cells were observed.
When considering the total monocyte population (classical +
intermediate + nonclassical), a significant temporal decline in
the percentage of KLRD1 high KLRB1 high ( −46.3%, P = 0.001),
KLRD1highPRF1high (−37.8%, P = 0.001), and KLRD1highGZMBhigh
(−54.1%, P = 0.0001) monocytes was also measured (Fig. 9, B to D).
This contrasted with temporal increases in the ratio of peripheral activated CD4 +CD45RA −FoxP3 high T reg among total T reg
in these HRS (61.7%, P = 0.0001; Fig. 9E). Further, there was a
significant temporal decrease in the ratio of mono4 monocytes
(KLRD1highKLRB1high, KLRD1highPRF1high, and KLRD1highGZMBhigh)
relative to activated T reg among progressors (Fig. 9E). A significant negative relationship between activated T regs (among total
Tregs) and mono4 (among total monocytes) for these participants
was observed when using any of these marker combinations
(KLRD1 highKLRB1high: r = −0.69, P = 0.002; KLRD1 highPRF1high:
r = −0.77, P = 0.003; and KLRD1 high GZMB high : r = −0.49,
P = 0.002).
We also conducted longitudinal analyses on 17 participants
that advanced from stage 0 to stage 1 T1D or stage 2 T1D (as
evidenced by presence of ≥2 islet autoantibodies, n = 15) or
from stage 0 to stage 3 T1D (clinical onset, n = 2). The mean age
of these participants was 9.0 ± 4.3 years, with a mean duration
of 4.2 ± 2.3 years between samplings. In these progressors, we
did not measure significant temporal changes in classical, inter mediate, nonclassical, and mono4 monocyte abundances; or the
ratio between the percentages of intermediate and nonclassical
monocytes; or activated T reg abundances (fig. S3, A to E). Over all, in contrast to T1D progression, these results indicate that
nonprogression is associated with temporal decreases in mono4
monocytes, increased percentages of activated T reg in the
periphery, and decreases in the intermediate- to- nonclassical
monocyte ratio.

DISCUSSION
Monocytes comprise a substantial component of human peripheral
blood, having essential functions in pathogen surveillance, phagocytosis, inflammation, and antigen presentation. While an under standing of monocyte heterogeneity and function in both health
and disease has progressively increased, the number of studies that
have examined monocytes in patients with T1D and their at- risk
relatives remain limited.
Here, we determined that monocytes of T1D family members
are hyperresponsive to TLR4 ligation, as evidenced by significantly
higher IL- 1β , TNF- α, and IL- 6 secretion after exposure to LPS
compared to uHC. This observation is consistent with prior reports
describing hypersecretion of IL- 1β and IL- 6 by monocytes of patients with T1D regardless of whether they were stimulated with
LPS or in an unperturbed resting state (17, 33). These findings are
relevant, as several clinical trials targeting proinflammatory cytokines have now provided proof of principle for these molecules as
effectors in T1D by improving measures of β cell function. These
trials include the targeting of TNF- α with etanercept (a TNF- α receptor fusion protein that fosters clearing of TNF- α from the circulation) and golimumab (an anti-TNF- α antibody) (34, 35). Overall,
trials of IL- 1β blockade with anakinra (IL1- RA) or canakinumab
(anti- IL1B antibody) did not slow β cell decline in ROT1D (36).
However, subgroup analysis of the anakinra study detected significantly higher stimulated C- peptide levels among IL1- RA-treated
participants having intermediate levels of β cell function at enrollment (36), and application of quantitative response analysis to the
treatment arm of the canakinumab trial suggested a positive effect
on retention of stimulated C- peptide (37). Further, our laboratory
reported an inverse relationship between measured inflammation
at onset and future stimulated C- peptide response in both anakinraand canakinumab- treated patients when using a GO- based inflammatory index (38). A case for proinflammatory cytokines playing
an effector role in T1D has grown through decades of study (39),
and the hypersecretion of cytokines by monocytes in T1D families
highlights the importance of innate immunity in T1D and its potential as a therapeutic target.
We also conducted gene expression studies of unstimulated
monocytes that included scRNA- seq, where in addition to elevated
LPS- stimulated cytokine expression, we associated this proinflammatory phenotype in T1D families with the expression of a cytolytic
transcriptome that is consistent with expansion of the previously
described mono4/NK- like subpopulation (18, 19). Compared to
uHC, cross- sectional flow cytometry analyses found that the mono4
population trended upward in healthy siblings of patients with T1D
and was significantly elevated in patients with ROT1D. Longitudinal
flow cytometry analyses of nonprogressors measured temporal decreases in the mono4 population that negatively correlated with increases in activated Treg. This immunoregulatory pattern contrasted
with T1D progressors, where neither temporal decrease in mono4
nor an age- dependent increase in active Treg was observed.
Among patients with ROT1D, the abundances of classical, intermediate, nonclassical, or mono4 monocytes were not found to
consistently correlate with HbA1c levels or insulin dose (table S6).
Further, classical and mono4 monocyte abundances did not correlate with measures of stimulated C- peptide AUC. However, we determined that higher abundances of intermediate monocytes near
clinical onset were associated with a more rapid decline in stimulated C- peptide over the subsequent 24- month study period
(Fig. 8A). Ren et al. (16) reported a significant increase in nonclassical monocytes when comparing uHC to ROT1D (19.5 ± −9.1 days
after onset). In contrast, we observed a significant decrease in
the nonclassical monocyte percentage when comparing the
ROT1D cohort (67.2 ± −34.2 days after onset) to the uHC cohort
(Fig. 6B). This difference may stem from our sampling of participants later in the immediate post- onset period, when patients
with ROT1D have generally achieved greater overall metabolic
stabilization (40). However, our analyses of LST1D controls exhibiting similar levels of glycemic control suggest that the altered
monocyte populations we observed in patients with ROT1D is not
related to elevated glucose levels. We also identified a direct relationship between the baseline percentage of nonclassical monocytes and the rate (slope) of C- peptide decline over the 24- month
study period (Fig. 8B). A more robust negative relationship was
observed with rate (slope) of C- peptide decline when monocyte
abundances were considered as a ratio between the percentages
of intermediate versus nonclassical subsets. The ability to predict
the persistence of C- peptide, particularly MMTT- stimulated
values ≥ 0.2 nM, is relevant as patients that retain β cell function
experience a reduced risk of severe hypoglycemia and microvascular
complications (41-45). This observation may offer a biomarker of
progression of immune- mediated β cell destruction and means to
stratify patients. It further suggests an anti- inflammatory role for
nonclassical monocytes in ROT1D, where their phagocytic removal
of damaged cells and debris from the vasculature may function to
promote wound healing and the resolution of inflammation (9 ).
Similar to Hamers et al. ( 46), our scRNA- seq analysis identified a
single CD16 + subset; however, their mass cytometry studies revealed multiple nonclassical monocyte subsets that exhibited
changes in frequency in the setting of severe coronary artery disease ( 46). Specifically, these authors identified a subset of Slan +
nonclassical monocytes that they postulated to be antiinflammatory and involved in the clearance of apoptotic cells (efferocytosis) and maintenance of vascular homeostasis (46).
Whether subset differences among nonclassical monocytes exist in
the context of T1D or T1D susceptibility is a limitation of this analysis that remains to be explored.
Similar to other studies that have used scRNA- seq to study monocyte populations, we observed heterogeneity extending beyond the
traditional tripartite classification scheme. Villani et al. ( 18) sequenced 372 monocytes of two healthy participants to define four
monocyte clusters, and Hu et al. (19) sequenced 11,259 monocytes
of three healthy participants to define six clusters. In this study, we
sequenced 13,915 monocytes of two uHC, two LRS, two, HRS and
two patients with ROT1D to define 10 monocyte clusters. We were
able to align 5 of 10 clusters with clusters identified by these prior
studies (cluster 3 aligned with classical monocytes, cluster 5 aligned
with nonclassical monocytes, cluster 0 aligned with S100A12 monocytes, cluster 4 aligned with HLA monocytes, and cluster 9 aligned
with mono4/NK- like monocytes). The presence of additional
unaligned clusters identified in this study may have stemmed from
(i) the sequencing of a greater number of monocytes and (ii) analyzing primarily participants with T1D susceptibility (patients with
T1D and unaffected siblings of patients with T1D). However, only
the mono4/NK- like signature expressed by cluster 9, which was evident in the microarray studies, showed enrichment among monocytes drawn from the sibling cohorts with inherited T1D risk and
even greater enrichment in the ROT1D cohort. Notably, not all

scRNA- seq studies of human monocytes have identified a mono4/
NK- like monocyte subpopulation (47, 48); authors of these studies
have suggested that this transcriptomic signature is derived from
physical doublets with NK cells. Here, steps were taken during
scRNA- seq to minimize the inclusion of doublets, and we validated
the presence of the mono4/NK- like monocyte population through a
flow cytometric analysis that excluded CD56+ NK cells and retained
HLA- DR+, CD14 +, CD16 +, KLRD1 high, KLRB1 high, and PRF1 high
monocytes. Villani et al. ( 18) observed that many monocytes defined as intermediate by surface markers coclustered in scRNA- seq
analysis with the classical or nonclassical subsets. In line with this
observation, we found the mono4 population distributed over the
classical, intermediate, and nonclassical populations when applying
a conventional rectangular gating strategy (Figs. 6 to 9).
While our studies find elevated abundances of mono4 monocytes in patients with ROT1D and find that their abundances decrease among nonprogressors, it is possible that the differentiation
of monocyte subpopulations is altered in patients with T1D, and the
expression of mono4 markers is a surrogate of this altered differentiation versus a driver of disease. We also recognize that the lack of
longitudinal transcriptional analyses is a limitation of this study.
However, in an elegant study, Xhonneux et al. (49) longitudinally
analyzed whole blood transcriptomes of >400 T1D progressors and
controls. The approach entailed RNA extraction from unfractionated
whole blood, gene expression measurements by microarray, and a
deconvolution approach, where the expression levels of immune
cell-specific transcripts were used to estimate cell- type frequencies.
The authors identified a signature consistent with NK cell enrichment that was significantly associated with both the development of
islet antibodies and progression to clinical T1D (49). Here, we did
not detect differences in the abundances of CD56+ NK cell populations in our cross- sectional flow cytometric analysis of the uHC,
LRS, HRS, LST1D, and ROT1D cohorts, nor in our longitudinal
analyses of T1D progressors and nonprogressors. The NK signature
defined by Xhonneux et al. ( 49) included 87 transcripts, notably
56 (64.4%; χ2, P < 1.0 × 10−5) of these transcripts were included in
the 826 transcript signature defined by our cross- sectional microarray analysis of negatively selected monocytes, and 30 (34.5%;
χ2, P < 1.0 × 10 −5) of these transcripts were included in the top
100 transcripts of cluster 9. We analyzed the scRNA- seq data for differential expression of this 87 transcript signatures between uHC,
LRS, HRS, and ROT1D participants for cells belonging to the NK
cluster (fig. S4). Our findings support the conclusion that the mono4
(cluster 9) population defined here, as well as the expression of
mono4 markers by monocyte populations defined by clusters 0 to
6 and 8 in patients with ROT1D (Fig. 4D), contributed to this prior
observation.
Overall, we find that circulating monocytes in T1D families
exhibit transcriptomes having elevated expression of cytolytic
and proinflammatory mediators, hyper- responsiveness to LPS
stimulation, and altered monocyte subset frequencies during progression and nonprogression. We observed increased mono4
abundances among patients with ROT1D and associated higher
intermediate- to- nonclassical monocyte ratios with a more rapid
decline in β cell functional mass during the post- onset period. We
also found that nonprogression is associated with temporal decreases in mono4 monocytes over 5 to 6 years coincident with
increases in the percentages of activated T regs in the periphery and
decreases in the intermediate- to- nonclassical monocyte ratio.
These findings align with prior observations that associate T1D
susceptibility and progression with a heightened immune response,
a propensity for T H1 responses toward insulin, and inflammation
before the onset of autoimmunity [reviewed in (50)]. Further
studies of monocyte subsets in the context of T1D progression/
nonprogression may lead to new therapeutic strategies and improved biomarkers of pre- or post- onset disease progression.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Experimental design
The objective of this study was to improve the understanding of
monocyte function and heterogeneity among subjects with and at
risk of developing T1D compared to uHC. Cross- sectional experimental assessments included measurement of cytokine release
after LPS stimulation, transcriptomic studies, and confirmation by
flow cytometry. The abundances of monocyte populations were
correlated with measures of functional β cell mass in newly diagnosed patients and examined among at risk progressors and nonprogressors.
Study participants
Participants were recruited through Children’s Wisconsin (CW) and
diagnosis of T1D was defined per World Health Organization criteria (51). ROT1D samples were collected from participants collected
2 to 7 months after diagnosis from participants with histories of
good glycemic control and had ≥ 1 autoantibody. Healthy control
participants were free of known infection at sample collection and
belonged to one of the following groups: (i) stage 0 siblings of probands with high- risk (DR3 and/or DR4) HLA genotypes (HRS), (ii)
stage 0 siblings of probands with lower risk (non- DR3/DR4) HLA
genotypes (LRS); and (iii) uHCs having no family history of T1D. The
sibling controls were unrelated to each other and the patients with
ROT1D studied. The LST1D cohort consisted of established patients
with T1D where samples were collected 6.1 to 29.7 years after diagnosis. The study participants are described in table S1. The study
procedures were approved by the CW Institutional Review Board
(#01-15 and #1171017- 6), and written informed consent was obtained from participants or their parents/legal guardians. Peripheral
blood samples were drawn into acid citrate dextrose solution A or
K+EDTA anticoagulant. Ficoll- Histopaque (Sigma- Aldrich, St. Louis,
MO, USA) density gradient centrifugation was used to separate
blood components immediately after draw, and plasma was stored at
−80°C until use. Autoantibody titers (GAD, IA2, insulin, and zinc
transporter 8) and class II HLA genotypes were determined as described (52, 53). MMTTs were conducted within in 100 days of diagnosis (baseline) and then at 6, 12, and 24 months after onset to
determine the rate of decline in stimulated C- peptide among patients with ROT1D as described (32, 54).
LPS- induced secretion of cytokines
Monocytes were negatively selected from cryopreserved PBMCs
using the EasySep Human Monocyte Enrichment Kit without CD16
depletion (STEMCELL Technologies Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA).
The recovered population was devoid of NK cells, as measured by
the absence of CD56 + cells by flow cytometry. A total of 100,000
monocytes per well were cultured for 24 hours at 37°C with LPS
(10 ng/ml) as described (55). The conditioned medium was recovered after centrifugation and stored at −80°C until IL- 1β, IL- 6, and

TNF- α levels were measured by enzyme- linked immunosorbent assay (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA).
Affymetrix GeneChip analyses of negatively
selected monocytes
RNA was extracted from monocytes negatively selected from PBMCs
as described above. Transcriptomes were measured using Affymetrix
GeneChip Human Genome U133 plus 2.0 arrays (Affymetrix, Santa
Clara, CA, USA) as described (29). Array data were subjected to
global median normalization with Bioconductor Robust Multi- array
Analysis (56). The significance of gene expression measurements was
determined by analysis of variance (ANOV A), and the rate of type I
errors in multiple testing was assessed by calculating FDR with
Partek Genomics Suite 7.2 (Partek, Saint Louis, MO, USA). Ontological analyses were conducted with the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis
package (Ingenuity Systems, Redwood City, CA, USA) and the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery
version 6.7 (57).
scRNA- seq of sorted PBMCs
Cryopreserved PBMC samples of eight age- matched participants
(two well- controlled patients with ROT1D, two HRS, two LRS, and
two uHC) were thawed and incubated with the fixable LIVE/
DEAD Violet dye (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY , USA) for
30 min on ice. The cells were subsequently stained with antibodies
targeting CD14 (clone M5E2, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ,
USA), CD16 (clone 3G8, BD Biosciences), HLA- DR (clone G46- 6, BD
Biosciences) for 30 min on ice and then washed and passed through a
30 μM filter. PBMCs were sorted with a FACSAria III cell sorter (BD
Biosciences), with specific forward and side scatter settings to exclude doublets. Using a hemocytometer and trypan blue staining, the
recovered HLA- DRhigh, CD14 +, CD16 +, and HLA- DRlow, CD14 −,
and CD16− cells were determined to have >40,000 cells per sample
and viability exceeding 90%. These two populations were mixed in a
1:1 ratio to generate a monocyte- enriched, single- cell, PBMC population. Single- cell Gel Beads- in- emulsions were prepared with the
Chromium Single Cell 3’v3 Reagent Kit (10X Genomics, Pleasanton,
CA) and a Chromium instrument. Single- cell capture, barcoded
reverse transcription, cell lysis, and library preparation were per formed as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Library sequencing used
a NovaSeq 6000 S2 Reagent kit (100 cycles; Illumina, San Diego,
CA, USA) and a NovaSeq Sequencing System (Illumina).
scRNA- seq data analysis
Raw sequencing data were processed using the 10X Genomics Cell
Ranger software (version 3.0.0) for sample demultiplexing, alignment to the human GRCh38.p14 reference, generating cell barcode,
and gene unique molecular index (UMI) count matrices. Preprocessing of the scRNA- seq data and downstream analysis was per formed using the Seurat package (version 4.3.0) (58) in R. To filter
low- quality cells or potential doublets, cells expressing fewer than
200 or more than 5000 genes or cells with more than 20% of UMI
counts coming from mitochondrial genes were removed from the
analysis. Centered log ratio transformation was used as the normalization method, and the 99% quantile was set as the threshold to
declare if a cell was considered positive. After quality control, a total
of 38,594 cells were retained for downstream analysis.
To integrate samples belonging to the four cohorts, we first lognormalized the count data and identified the top 2000 most variable
transcripts (using method vst) for each cohort. We used a canonical
correlation analysis-based algorithm to remove batch effect across
four cohorts (by using function IntegrateData). Integrated data were
further scaled with removal of variation from total UMI counts and
percentage of mitochondrial genes. To reduce dimensionality and
denoise the data, PCA was performed on the top 2000 variable genes.
Shared nearest neighbor of each cell was constructed using first 30 PCs,
and different cell clusters were identified using the Louvain- Jaccard
graph- based algorithm. We used t- SNE with 30 PCs as input to visualize the data in two- dimensional space along with cluster results from
the clustering algorithm. Identification of differentially expressed
genes between groups of cells was performed by Wilcoxon rank sum
tests. Cluster marker genes were declared with adjusted P value less
than 0.05 (with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing) and were
used to define well- known PBMC populations.
A recluster analysis focusing on monocytes was performed by
first retrieving the cells from clusters 0 and 7 (Fig. 3), the aforementioned normalization was reperformed, and the batch correction
method was used to integrate data from the four cohorts. This was
followed by dimension reduction through PCA using the first 10 PCs
as input for UMAP to visualize the data in two- dimensional space.
Annotations were confirmed with R package Azimuth (25) using
the human PBMC reference. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was
performed with R package Monocle3 (59).
Dimension reduction plots (t- SNE and UMAP), heatmaps, violin plots, dot plots, and feature plots were generated using functions
DimPlot, DoHeatmap, VlnPlot, DotPlot, and FeaturePlot in Seurat,
respectively. Bar plots were generated using the R package ggplot2
(version 3.4.2). The AddModuleScore function in Seurat was used to
calculate gene module scores.
Analysis of monocyte subsets by flow cytometry
Cryopreserved PBMCs (~ 3 × 106 PBMCs per participant) were
thawed, then incubated with the fixable LIVE/DEAD Violet dye (Life
Technologies, Grand Island, NY , USA) for 30 min on ice, and then
stained with fluorochrome- conjugated monoclonal antibodies tar geting CD3 (clone UCHT1, BD Biosciences), CD19 (clone HIB19,
BD Biosciences), CD14 (clone M5E2, BD Biosciences), CD16 (clone
3G8; BD Biosciences), HLA- DR (clone G46- 6, BD Biosciences),
CD56 (clone B159, BD Biosciences), KLRB1/CD161(clone HP-
3G10, BD Biosciences), and KLRD1/CD94 (clone HP- 3D9, BD Biosciences) on ice for 30 min. Next, cells were washed, fixed, and
permeabilized for intracellular staining using the FIX & PERM Cell
Permeabilization Kit (GAS004, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in accor dance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Then, PBMCs were stained
with anti- PRF1 (clone B- D48, BioLegend), and anti- GZMB (clone
GB11, BD Biosciences) for 45 min. Treg populations were stained as
previously described (29) using anti- CD4 (clone RPA- T4), anti- CD25
(clone M- A251), anti- CD45RO (clone UCHL1), anti- CD45RA
(clone HI100), anti- CD127 (clone HIR- 7R- M21) (BD Bioscience,
San Jose, CA), and anti- FOXP3 (clone PCH101) (eBioscience, San
Diego, CA). Representative flow cytometry gating plots for the T cell
staining protocol are shown infig. S5.
The stained PBMCs were analyzed on a Cytek Aurora flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences) using five laser line configurations, blue
(488 nm), red (640 nm), yellow green (561 nm), violet (405 nm) and
ultraviolet (355 nm) with Spectro Flo software v2.2.0.2, which automatically calculated compensation by unmixing the cells and cor recting spectral spillover using the single stained reference controls

for each fluorescent tag, and unstained control with autofluorescence
extraction option was enabled. Further, to minimize the unmixing error, the spectrum profiles for all single stained reference controls
were reconciled against the expected spectrum profiles provided by
the Cytek Full Spectrum Viewer (https://spectrum.cytekbio.com/).
